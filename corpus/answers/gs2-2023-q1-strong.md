The decline in President's Rule since the mid-1990s is best explained as a judicial
constraint that hardened into a political norm. Both halves matter: the law narrowed
the discretion, and coalition politics removed much of the incentive to use what
discretion remained.

The legal turn is {{f:S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)|S. R. Bommai v. Union of
India (1989)}}. The Court held that a proclamation under {{f:Article 356|Article 365}}
is subject to judicial review, that the floor of the House rather than the Governor's
subjective satisfaction is the test of majority, and that a dissolved Assembly can be
revived if the proclamation is struck down. The last point is what gave the ruling
teeth, because it removed the certainty that dismissal was irreversible.

The political factors are equally important. The end of single-party dominance after
{{f:1989|1996}} produced Union governments dependent on regional allies, and a party
that could be dismissed in one State had every reason to resist the precedent in
another. The Sarkaria Commission's recommendation that Article 356 be used only as a
last resort acquired force once no single party could ignore it.

However, the norm should not be mistaken for a prohibition. Proclamations have
continued, and the Governor's discretion in inviting a leader to form a government
remains largely unreviewed. What changed is the cost of misuse, not its possibility —
which is the characteristic way constitutional conventions actually operate in India.
