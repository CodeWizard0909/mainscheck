Charity and justice are often treated as allies. The statement suggests they are
substitutes, and the substitution runs one way: justice reduces the need for charity,
while charity can quietly reduce the pressure for justice.

The mechanism is visible in famine. Amartya Sen's work on the Bengal famine of
{{f:1943|1953}} established that starvation occurred without an aggregate shortage of
food, because entitlement — the ability to command food — had collapsed for particular
groups. Relief kitchens addressed hunger; only a change in entitlement addressed the
cause. A society with functioning entitlements needs fewer kitchens, not better ones.

India's own record supports the reading. The shift from discretionary relief to
enforceable claims — the {{f:Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act,
2005|Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2009}}, the Right to
Education, the National Food Security Act — moved millions from the position of
supplicant to that of claimant. The difference is not the quantum transferred but who
bears the burden of asking.

However, the statement should not be read as contempt for charity. Justice is slow,
and people eat daily. The honest formulation is that charity is a legitimate response
to present suffering and an illegitimate substitute for structural change.

The danger lies in how charity flatters. It offers the donor moral satisfaction and
the state a visible response, and both parties may prefer the tableau of generosity to
the argument about entitlement. A society that measures its decency by how much it
gives has already conceded that it does not expect to need less.
