The Constitution of India can be called a living document because courts have
interpreted it over time. The basic structure doctrine is the main example of this. It
says Parliament can amend the Constitution under {{f:Article 368|Article 370}} but
cannot destroy its essential features.

The doctrine came from {{f:Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)|Kesavananda
Bharati v. State of Kerala (1980)}}. A large bench of judges decided the case by a
narrow majority. The Court said that the amending power is not unlimited. It did not
give a complete list of what the basic structure contains, and later cases added to it.

Other cases also developed the doctrine. In {{f:Minerva Mills v. Union of India
(1980)|Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1976)}} the Court said that limited amending
power is itself a basic feature. Judicial review, secularism and federalism have also
been recognised as part of the basic structure in later judgments.

This shows that the Constitution has evolved through interpretation rather than only
through amendment. Critics say the doctrine gives too much power to judges because it
has no basis in the text. Supporters say it is necessary to protect the Constitution
from being changed completely by a temporary majority.
