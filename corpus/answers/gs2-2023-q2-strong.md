The constitutional approach to gender justice has moved from formal equality towards
substantive equality, and the shift is visible in both text and interpretation.

The textual basis is a cluster rather than a single provision.
{{f:Article 14|Article 19}} guarantees equality before law, Article 15(1) forbids
discrimination on grounds of sex, and Article 15(3) permits special provisions for
women — an explicit acknowledgement that identical treatment of unequals reproduces
inequality. Article 16(2) extends this to public employment, Article 39(a) and (d)
direct the State towards equal livelihood and equal pay, and
{{f:Article 42|Article 44}} obliges it to secure just and humane conditions of work
and maternity relief.

Case law supplies the content. {{f:Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997)|Vishaka v.
State of Rajasthan (1992)}} read workplace sexual harassment into Articles 14, 19 and
21 and framed binding guidelines in the absence of legislation. Joseph Shine struck
down adultery as an offence premised on a woman's subordination, and Secretary,
Ministry of Defence v. Babita Puniya rejected physiological generalisation as a basis
for denying permanent commission.

However, the record is uneven. The Uniform Civil Code under Article 44 remains a
directive rather than a duty, and personal laws continue to govern succession and
marriage for most Indians. Constitutional gender justice has therefore advanced
furthest where the State is the actor and least where the family is.
