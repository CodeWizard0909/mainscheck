Artificial Intelligence refers to systems that perform tasks ordinarily requiring human
inference — pattern recognition, prediction, classification — by learning statistical
structure from data rather than by following rules written in advance.

In clinical diagnosis its contribution is strongest where the task is perceptual and
the ground truth is recorded. Image-based screening for diabetic retinopathy and
tuberculosis has shown that a model can triage at a volume no radiologist roster can
match, which matters more in India than in better-staffed systems because the
constraint is specialist availability rather than imaging capacity. AI additionally
assists in flagging deterioration from routine vitals, where the signal is present but
too gradual for intermittent human observation.

The threat to privacy is real and structurally different from ordinary data risk.
Health data is not merely sensitive but inferentially rich: a model trained on scans
can reveal attributes a patient never disclosed, and de-identification offers weaker
protection than it appears because re-identification improves as auxiliary datasets
grow. The {{f:Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023|Digital Personal Data
Protection Act, 2020}} establishes consent obligations, however consent is a thin
safeguard where refusing it means declining treatment.

The honest position is therefore conditional. AI in diagnosis is justified where its
error profile is published, its training population resembles the patients it will
see, and a clinician remains accountable for the decision. Absent those, it transfers
risk from the system to the patient while appearing to reduce it.
