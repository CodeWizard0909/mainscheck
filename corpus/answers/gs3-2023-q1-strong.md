Manufacturing has been stuck near a sixth of GDP for two decades, and the binding
constraint is not policy attention but the difficulty MSMEs face in growing past the
threshold where scale economies begin.

Present policy works on three fronts. The Production Linked Incentive scheme ties
support to incremental output rather than to capacity created, which is a sound design
choice because it pays for results rather than intent. Credit access has been widened
through the {{f:Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme|Emergency Credit Linked
Guarantee Scheme}} and collateral-free lending under CGTMSE. The revised MSME
definition of {{f:2020|2018}}, which added turnover alongside investment, removed one
perverse incentive to stay small.

The gaps are structural. PLI is concentrated in capital-intensive sectors where large
firms dominate, so its benefit reaches MSMEs only indirectly through supply chains
that may not exist yet. Delayed payments remain the single most cited constraint, and
a statutory 45-day rule helps little when the buyer is the more powerful party.
Compliance burden is additionally regressive, since a fixed cost falls harder on a
smaller firm.

Faster growth therefore requires less new incentive and more removal of friction:
predictable contract enforcement, working capital that does not depend on collateral,
and a power supply firms can plan around. Incentives can raise output in a chosen
sector; only these determine whether a small firm chooses to become a medium one.
