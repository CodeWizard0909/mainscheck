The Indian Constitution has never been a static text. Its evolution has been driven
as much by judicial interpretation as by the {{f:one hundred and six amendments|forty
amendments}} passed by Parliament, and the basic structure doctrine sits at the centre
of that evolution. The doctrine holds that Parliament's amending power under
{{f:Article 368|Article 386}} is wide but not unlimited: it may not alter the
Constitution's essential features.

The doctrine emerged from a sequence of conflicts over property rights and
parliamentary supremacy. In {{f:Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)|Kesavananda
Bharati v. State of Kerala (1967)}}, a thirteen-judge bench held by a narrow majority
that while Parliament could amend any provision, it could not damage the
Constitution's basic structure. The Court deliberately declined to define that
structure exhaustively, leaving it to be filled in case by case — a choice that has
been criticised as granting the judiciary an open-ended veto, and defended as the only
workable response to a question no drafting committee could settle in advance.

Subsequent cases gave the doctrine content. {{f:Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain
(1975)|Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1978)}} applied it to strike down a
constitutional amendment shielding the Prime Minister's election from judicial review,
establishing free and fair elections as part of the basic structure. {{f:Minerva Mills
v. Union of India (1980)|Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1985)}} held that limited
amending power was itself basic, and restored the balance between Fundamental Rights
and Directive Principles.

The doctrine's living quality is clearest in what it has come to protect. Judicial
review, federalism, secularism and the rule of law were not enumerated in 1973; they
were recognised over five decades of adjudication. This is the mechanism by which a
document drafted for one India continues to govern another.

Yet describing the Constitution as living should not obscure the cost. The doctrine
has no textual basis, and its content is determined by an unelected court. Its
legitimacy rests less on constitutional text than on a settled understanding that some
questions should be placed beyond majoritarian revision. That understanding has held
for fifty years, which is itself the strongest argument in its favour.
