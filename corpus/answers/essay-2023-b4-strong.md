The aphorism is usually quoted as praise for character over content. Read more
carefully, it is a claim about what survives forgetting — and what survives is method,
disposition and the capacity to be taught again.

The evidence is ordinary. A graduate forgets the derivation of a formula within a year
and retains the habit of asking whether a claim has been tested. The facts decay
because they were never the durable part; what persists is the shape the mind was bent
into while acquiring them. This is why the content of a curriculum matters less than
whether it was learned through reasoning or through reproduction.

India's examination culture makes the point painfully. A system that rewards recall
produces exactly the learning that does not survive, because nothing was built except
retrieval. The National Education Policy of {{f:2020|2016}} identifies this, proposing
competency-based assessment, however a policy cannot legislate away an incentive that
parents, coaching institutes and employers all reinforce.

The statement can nevertheless be pushed too far. Forgetting is not costless, and the
romantic version of this idea has been used to excuse teaching very little. A physician
who has forgotten anatomy is not left with the essence of medicine; they are left
unable to practise. Method without substance is as empty as substance without method.

What remains, then, is not a residue but an apparatus: the ability to read something
difficult, to notice when one does not understand, and to find out. School's real
product is a person who can still learn at forty. Everything else is scaffolding, and
scaffolding is meant to come down.
