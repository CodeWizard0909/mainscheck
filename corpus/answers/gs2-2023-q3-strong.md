e-governance has delivered most where a transaction could be standardised, and least
where governance requires discretion. That distinction explains nearly every
inadequacy worth naming.

The gains are real. Direct Benefit Transfer moved subsidy delivery onto a rails-based
system, and {{f:the Right to Information Act, 2005|the Right to Information Act,
2009}} combined with online disclosure has made routine records retrievable without a
petition. Effectiveness improved because the intermediary who once rationed access was
removed from the process.

The inadequacies begin with the assumption of connectivity and capability. Digital
access remains uneven across rural, elderly and disabled users, so a portal-only
service can exclude precisely the citizens a welfare scheme targets. Authentication
failures in biometric systems are not merely technical faults; they translate into
denial of entitlement, and the burden of proving the failure falls on the claimant.

Transparency is also narrower than it appears. Publishing data is not the same as
publishing decisions, and the reasoning behind a discretionary order is rarely what
gets uploaded. Accountability suffers additionally because grievance redress is often
the least funded component of a digital rollout, leaving no effective remedy when the
system errs.

The deeper problem is that digitisation frequently encodes an existing process rather
than reforming it. A badly designed procedure made faster is still badly designed, and
the speed can make its defects harder to contest.
